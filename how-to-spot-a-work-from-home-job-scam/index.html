How to Spot a Work-From-Home Job Scam

The warning signs of fake remote job offers, from crypto deposits to fake interviews, based on FBI alerts, plus what to do if you already paid.

Remote Work NotesOct 11, 2026 · 5 min read

A remote job search means applying to companies you will never visit, interviewing with people you may never meet, and accepting offers by email. That is normal now, and scammers know it. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) has issued public warnings about two kinds of fake remote jobs that keep recurring. Knowing how each one works makes the red flags easy to spot.

Scam type 1: The "task" job that asks you to pay

In a June 2024 alert, the FBI described scammers offering fake work-from-home jobs built around simple, repetitive tasks, such as rating restaurants or "optimizing" a service by repeatedly clicking a button. The scammers pose as a legitimate business, such as a staffing or recruiting agency, and often reach out first with an unsolicited call or message.

The trap is the pay structure. According to the FBI, the job is designed to be confusing, and it requires you to make cryptocurrency payments to earn more money or to "unlock" more work. Those payments go straight to the scammer. Meanwhile, you are shown a fake dashboard that appears to show your earnings growing, but none of that money can actually be withdrawn.

The FBI lists three red flags for this scheme:

  • You are told to make cryptocurrency payments to your employer as part of the job.
  • The job description uses the word "optimization" and involves relatively simple tasks.
  • Nobody asks for references during hiring.

The core rule is simple: a real employer pays you. If a job requires you to send money in order to keep working, it is not a job.

Scam type 2: The fake listing that harvests your identity

An earlier FBI alert describes a slower, more convincing scheme. Criminals create a website with a domain name that looks like a real company's, post fake openings on popular job boards, and send applicants to the copycat site. Victims are then interviewed, often over a teleconference app, by people posing as recruiters, HR staff and hiring managers.

After the "interview," the victim gets a work-at-home offer and sometimes a contract to sign. Then come the requests: a copy of a driver's license, Social Security number, direct deposit details and credit card information. The scammers may also ask for upfront payment for background checks, training, equipment or supplies, often promising reimbursement in the first paycheck. Once the money arrives, the FBI says, communication stops. Victims in that alert reported an average loss of nearly $3,000, plus damage to their credit.

The FBI's list of warning signs for this version:

  • Interviews are not held in person or over a secure video call.
  • The interview app uses an email address rather than a phone number.
  • The "employer" contacts you from a non-company email domain.
  • You are asked to buy start-up equipment from the company.
  • You are asked for credit card information.
  • The job appears on job boards but not on the company's own website.
  • The recruiter or manager has no profile on the job board, or a profile that does not fit the role.

A checklist before you accept a remote offer

Most of these checks take a few minutes and come straight from the FBI's protective tips.

  1. Find the company yourself. Search for the company by name only, not through a link in the message. The FBI notes that seeing multiple websites for what should be one company (for example, a name with and without "llc" added) can indicate a fake listing.
  2. Confirm the job exists on the company's own careers page. A posting that exists only on a job board is a warning sign.
  3. Check where the email came from. Recruiters at real companies usually write from the company's domain.
  4. Never pay to get or keep a job. The FBI's advice is direct: never send money to an alleged employer, and never send money by wire transfer to someone you met online.
  5. Hold back sensitive details until you are actually hired. The FBI explains that legitimate companies ask for personal and bank account information for payroll after hiring, and that a video call can help confirm identity if meeting in person is not possible.
  6. Do not rely on "https" alone. The FBI points out that scammers can use secure-looking web addresses too, so the padlock icon does not prove a site is legitimate.
  7. Be careful with unsolicited messages. Avoid clicking links, downloading files or opening attachments in job offers you did not ask for.

If you think you already fell for one

Act quickly. The FBI recommends these steps:

  • Contact your bank or card issuer immediately and ask them to stop or reverse the transactions. Ask them to contact the receiving bank as well.
  • Report it to the IC3 at ic3.gov, including any transaction details you have, or to your local FBI field office.
  • Report the listing to the job site where you found it, and tell the company whose name was used.
  • Do not pay anyone who promises to recover your losses. The FBI's 2024 alert specifically warns against paying services that claim they can recover lost cryptocurrency.

If you shared your Social Security number or bank details, treat it as a possible identity theft situation and monitor your accounts closely.

Why these scams work

Both schemes copy the real remote hiring process closely: online postings, video interviews, digital paperwork. That is exactly why the FBI notes that hiring scams are hard to identify until it is too late. The difference is always in the direction the money moves. Real employers do not charge you for equipment, training, background checks or the right to work, and they do not pay you in a dashboard you cannot withdraw from.

Key takeaways

  • The FBI warns of fake "task" jobs that require cryptocurrency deposits and show fake earnings you cannot withdraw.
  • Fake listings on job boards can lead to realistic interviews designed to collect your ID, Social Security number and bank details.
  • Red flags include paying to work, no reference checks, non-company email addresses and jobs missing from the company's own website.
  • Share payroll details only after you have verified the employer and been hired.
  • If you paid, contact your bank right away, report to ic3.gov, and ignore anyone offering paid recovery services.
